## 3. Prompting that works: Aditya (merged in PR #6, condensed here)

Full detail in `prompting-section-outline.md` (v4, Nov 10) and in `final/plan.md` after PR #6. Facilitator bullets only:
- What prompting is: a very smart person who does not know you. Your job is to point them at your problem.
- Four parts: context, intent, format, restrictions, plus one "good looks like" line. That line is the core skill.
- One example, built live: "Write an email to a client about the delay" (Round 0, weak), then add context and intent (Round 1), define good together (Round 2: "Under 120 words. Opens with the decision and the new date. Names the one thing the client must do, and by when. No detail I did not give you. Ends with the question I need answered."), then add format and restrictions (Round 3). Every clause in the "good looks like" line can be ticked yes or no in ten seconds; that is the standard. Show weak and strong side by side. No client name, no real number anywhere.
- Six habits, from Anthropic's Prompting best practices: give context and say why; be specific, including what good looks like; say what to do, not what not to do; show an example when format matters; give it a role in one sentence; ask it to check itself.
- Two phone tricks: talk instead of type for a 60-second brain-dump; end with "Ask me the questions you need answered before you start" or "Improve my prompt, then run it."
- Close: attendees circle one habit on the back of the prompt card, next to the six habits, and write it in one line. That is the habit they use in the combined exercise.
- Prep: run these prompts in a real chat app before the event and save the outputs as screenshots, so the segment needs no Wi-Fi.

## 4. Combined exercise: one email, in Muse: Aditya + Vinit (filled, Aditya to review)

One continuous flow, 20 minutes, one participant-selected task throughout: prompting, generation, review, revision, and workflow mapping. Email is the default, not the only allowed task. If you do not have an email, pick one of four weekly tasks: a tricky email or message; rough meeting notes into a clean summary; prep for a meeting or call; a short proposal, plan, or status update. Worksheet 2 below carries it.

- **Part 1, prompt it, 14:00-19:00 (5 min).** Use a real email you need to send on Monday, sanitized. Write the prompt on the worksheet with the four parts and a "good looks like" line. End every prompt with "Keep it under 150 words." No names, clients, or numbers; use "Client A," "$X," "my manager." The real email is what makes the judge matter, and it is what you take home with you.
- **Part 2, get your briefing, then draft the top one, 19:00-24:00 (5 min).** Ask Muse to look at your 3 most recent emails in your own inbox and give you a briefing for each one: what do I need to know, when do I need to know it, and what do I need to do next? If you did not connect your inbox, use the Jordan Lee demo or the 3 sample emails on the board. If Muse is not logged in, run it in any free chat app you already use. Then draft the most urgent one. Draft only. Stop before sending.
- **Part 3, evaluate it, 24:00-29:00 (5 min).** Swap with your partner. They make one change: add missing context, sharpen the "good" line, or add "lead with the decision" or "what should I verify?" Then the blind judge: the owner holds their own phone and shows both drafts on screen, marked only "left" and "right," to a judge from the next pair, with the "good looks like" line. The judge never takes the phone. The judge views both on the phone, then scores on the worksheet: which one they would send, left or right, and why, in one line. Then the judge stretch, 60 seconds: the judge has to name the one change that would make the losing version win. That forces the judge to use the "good looks like" line properly, instead of just picking the nicer-sounding email. Then read the winning draft out loud to your table, and a short sharing session: one thing each table learned, table captain reads it out. Show of hands: improved, original, or could not tell. If the original won, that is a good story too: more is not always better, and the "good" line is what decides.
- **Part 4, turn it into an agent, 29:00-34:00 (5 min).** Design the agent for the same chore, in four boxes on the worksheet: Trigger → AI step → Human approval → Destination. Email first: summarize your emails, then draft / reply to 1 email. Write one value line ("saves me 30 minutes a week") and one implications line ("it can read my inbox, so I will give it read-only, and I will check before it sends"). Facilitator shows the Muse permission screen on the synthetic account here, and stops before sending. **Stretch for fast finishers, no second exercise:** add one constraint to the agent, for example "it must never send without me, and it must flag any email from a client I have not met." That keeps the room together instead of half the room starting a new exercise while the other half is still judging.

If phones or Wi-Fi fail: rank four pre-printed prompts weakest to strongest on paper, or spot the missing part (label C, I, F, R, G on your partner's prompt and add the missing one). Skip running the original and run only the improved prompt to save 30 seconds.

## 5. Evaluation and guardrails: Vinit (filled)

- **Evaluation, 34:00-37:00 (3 min).** "You just ran a blind test. That is how the leaderboards rank AI models." LMArena ranks models by blind human votes; attendees have just done one. AI slop spot check: generic content, invented detail, unnecessary length, and output that should be deleted rather than fixed. Score your Exercise tool on five checks: does it do the job, is the output accurate, is it fast enough, is the access narrow enough, would you trust it with this task again?
- **Guardrails, 37:00-40:00 (3 min).** Golden rule: personal data, financials, and unreleased work never go into a public tool. Main story: Samsung, 2023, employees pasting confidential code and meeting notes into ChatGPT, then Samsung restricting it. Green / yellow / red sort on five items, tables call out the colour. Muse permission screen on the synthetic account: show read-only vs draft vs send, audit trail, disconnect. Anyone who connected their own inbox disconnects it here, shown step by step. Verify before you send: recall *Mata v. Avianca* (lawyers sanctioned for filing nonexistent ChatGPT cases) and *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (company held responsible for its chatbot's wrong fare). You own anything that leaves your desk.

## 6. Wrap-up and 30-day plan: Vinit (filled)

- One line each: "My next step is ___ , by ___ ."
- 30-day plan, one task a week, introduced one at a time: Week 1 drafting; Week 2 summarizing or research; Week 3 repetitive-task mapping. Measure it: minutes saved or quality improved. Review every output.
- Tell your manager what changed, with one number, for example a report that took 2 hours now takes 40 minutes.
- The take-home commitment and the collected evaluation are separate. Worksheet 1, the prompt card, is the take-home: attendees keep it, with their prompt, their habit, and their next step on it. Worksheet 3, the evaluation and exit card, is collected at the door. Exit card, target 80%+: (1) a personal task with a chosen tool and a written "good looks like" definition; (2) one improvement with a measure; (3) a value and implications line, with access narrowed; (4) one next step. Disconfirmed if most cards stop at step 1, or most say "allow everything."
